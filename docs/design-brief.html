Design brief · v3 · built on calendar evidence, not self-report

Two Days a Week

An app that campaigns for Monday and Friday on Singapore hours at your December review, and in the meantime stops the pre-shift window leaking away.

The principle everything else follows from

You've tried calendar blocking. You've tried streaks. Both failed. They have the same flaw: they are promises you make to yourself, and you are allowed to break them. A block you wrote is a block you can ignore. A streak you own is a streak you can drop.

So the design rule is: prefer constraints other people hit over promises you make to yourself. A closed booking slot works because someone else runs into it and reschedules. A person expecting an answer works because their expectation isn't yours to cancel. That distinction sorts every feature below into "will work for you" and "won't".

What this rules in and out

Out
Writing intentions into your calendar. Tried, abandoned. A self-authored event has no authority over you.
Out
Streaks and chains. They collapse on the first miss and take the habit with them.
Out
Policing your early work creep. Not needed — see below, the creep has a mechanical cause and a mechanical fix.
In
Closing your booking availability. Other people hit it. You cannot quietly override it at 9am.
In
A bot that expects a reply, and a partner who sees the weekend plan. Both are outside your own willpower.
In
Rotation with guaranteed weekly slots. Survives a bad week without collapsing, unlike a streak.

The fastest win available, and it needs nobody's permission

Your Bookings page is open wide enough that people book you at 08:00, 08:30, 09:00 and 09:30 Singapore time. On Tuesday 28 July, four landed between 08:00 and 10:30 — that morning was simply gone, and not to drift.

These are good meetings — Goldman Sachs, Robin Hanson, Alex Tabarrok — so the answer isn't to stop taking them. It's to cap the morning slots, as you chose: keep a defined number each week for genuine APAC and US contacts, and close the rest. One settings change, roughly a third of your morning problem, this week.

Your bot never ran

stu-time-bot from February is well-formed: a four-week rota, WhatsApp nudges, a GitHub Actions cron at 11:00 SGT. But .github/workflows is a file, not a directory. Actions only reads YAML inside that folder, so the workflow was never registered and has never fired.

You didn't lose motivation for that bot. It never spoke to you once. That matters for what you conclude about yourself: the evidence you've been reading as "I don't stick at things" includes at least one case that was a path bug.

The campaign

December review, date still to fix. Your manager doesn't know this is coming, you have two years of trust with them, and they are persuaded by relationship rather than by documents. Two objections: no precedent, and output might drop.

You already have your precedent

next.io runs a company-wide “Focus Wednesday — No Meetings”, organised by Ana, covering the whole company. Your organisation has already accepted, in writing and at scale, that protecting time from meetings is legitimate and improves output.

You are not asking to invent something. You're asking to apply an existing company principle to a person whose timezone makes it matter more. That is a much smaller thing to say yes to.

The softening move comes first

You have a regular 1:1 and your manager is cold on this. The review must not be the first they hear of it — a December surprise invites a defensive no. Raise it lightly in a 1:1 well beforehand, so that by December you are confirming a known conversation rather than opening one.

The meeting audit is the whole case

Delivered work is the wrong strand for you: projects and deliverables move on long cycles, and sixteen weeks won't produce enough completions to baseline. Meetings will — you have plenty.

The question the audit answers is narrow and decisive: what UK time do your meetings actually sit at? Your shift covers UK 08:00–15:00. From the sample, much of your genuinely collaborative load sits in the London morning. If that holds across a full quarter, then Monday and Friday on Singapore hours costs London far less than it sounds like — and you'll be bringing your manager a finding rather than a demand, which is exactly the register that works on them.

One thing to keep out of the case

You mentioned seeing your partner in gaps between meetings during the shift. Never let that into the conversation. “I have gaps in my evening” is heard as spare capacity, and it argues the opposite of your case.

The overlap you're asking London to give up

The week

You want ambition and enjoyment held in tension, with neither crowding the other out, and you want guaranteed slots rather than a reported ratio. So the week is a fixed shape, and an unused enjoyment slot is reported as a miss exactly like an unused project block.

Discovery is a real feature, not a nice-to-have

You and your partner both like a productive weekend and wake early to get the most of it, and you want to find new places in Singapore and nearby. That's specific enough to build: a shared backlog of places, filtered by how long they take and how far they are, that turns a drifting Saturday into a decided one. It's also the piece your partner is genuinely part of, alongside training — which is where you said a person works and a bot doesn't.

The project problem

You have 36 repositories. os-inspiration, os-inspiration-2, tim-os, next-os, Headroom, ai-radar, tim-time, stu-time-bot. You named the problem yourself: it isn't a shortage of ideas, it's that starting is easy and finishing isn't.

You asked for the pattern shown rather than policed, so: no rule, one number. Repos started, repos untouched for ninety days, and how long since the last thing you actually finished. Visible whenever you're about to start something new. No blocking, no permission, no lecture — just the count, at the moment it's relevant.

Which does raise the obvious point about this app. If it ships and then joins the list, it has demonstrated your thesis rather than solved it. That argues for building the smallest thing that could work and running it for a month before adding anything.

Build order

  1. Close the booking window. Not code — a settings change, this week. Biggest immediate return, no dependencies, no permission needed.
  2. The meeting audit. Your calendar is already the dataset and it's already readable. This is the case, and every unmeasured week is missing from the baseline.
  3. The Telegram check-in. A morning message that asks what your first action is and expects a reply; an evening one asking whether it happened. Small, and it's the mechanic you said works on you.
  4. The week view with guaranteed slots and rotation.
  5. The discovery backlog, shared with your partner.
  6. The repo counter. One number, shown at the right moment.

Items 1 and 2 deliver most of the value and neither is really an app. Worth noticing before we build one.
