Two Days a Week

An app that runs a campaign to win two Singapore-hours days at your year-end review — and, in the meantime, makes the eight free hours you already have every weekday count.

What I got wrong the first time

I built a planner: block out the day, set weekly targets, tick things off. Then you told me calendar blocking and streak trackers are both things you have already tried and abandoned. The prototype was a competent version of two mechanics with a proven track record of failing you.

The deeper miss: I treated your evening shift as a fixed constraint to optimise around. It isn't. It's the thing you most want to change, you have a specific ask, and you have a date to make it.

Three mechanics are now off the table

Cut
Booking intentions as calendar events. You did this and started ignoring the alerts. An event you wrote yourself carries no authority.
Cut
Streaks. They work until you break one, then the whole structure loses its grip. Anything I build has to survive a bad week without collapsing.
Cut
Policing early work creep. You called it a symptom of an unclaimed morning rather than a cause. Fill the window and it stops on its own.

The thing worth noticing

You currently have about eight free hours every weekday — substantially more discretionary time than someone on normal hours. The ask you want to make would reduce that. Nine-to-six leaves you roughly one free hour before work and an evening.

So the two days aren't really about getting more time. They're about getting time that is synchronised with everybody else — when gyms have classes, friends are free, shops and clinics are open, and a Friday flight to Bangkok is a weekend rather than a logistical problem.

Which means the daily half is not just a stopgap

It is the test of your own hypothesis. If eight unsynchronised hours a day can't be made to feel worthwhile, that's worth knowing before you reorganise your career around getting fewer, better-timed ones. And if you do make them count, the log of that is the single most persuasive thing you can put in front of your manager in December.

The case: two objections, one strategy

You named the blockers precisely: no precedent, and output might drop. They need different answers.

ObjectionWhy it bitesThe answer
No precedent Your manager isn't judging you, they're setting policy. A yes creates a rule for everyone. Ask for a pilot, not a change. A bounded eight-week trial with a review date isn't a precedent, it's an experiment. It is a far smaller thing to say yes to, and it converts the question from "should this be allowed" to "did it work".
Output might drop It's a forecast about the future, and forecasts can't be argued with — only pre-empted. Baseline it now. Start measuring before you ask, so you arrive with months of data rather than a promise. Without a baseline you cannot prove a non-drop, because there's nothing to compare against.

The baseline is the part that has a deadline attached, and it's the reason not to wait. Every week you don't measure is a week missing from the before-and-after.

The overlap question

You chose to ask for standard Singapore hours with a promise to be reachable if something urgent lands. That's your call and the design assumes it. But you should walk into the conversation knowing exactly what you're asking London to give up, because they will do this arithmetic even if you don't.

So: open where you want to land, but bring the fallback

Open with 09:00–18:00 and reachable-if-urgent, as you chose. Keep 11:00–20:00 in your pocket as the concession you make if coverage is the sticking point — it costs you the late evening but keeps almost everything you actually want, and conceding it makes the yes easier to give.

One warning on reachable if urgent: undefined, it quietly becomes always-on and you end up working both patterns. Define it — a channel, a response window, what counts as urgent — and put that definition in the proposal rather than leaving it to be discovered.

Why one app and not two

The campaign and the daily half aren't neighbours, they feed each other. That loop is the reason this is a single product.

The campaign half

  • A countdown to the review date. Not decoration — the milestone plan runs backwards from it, so every week has a job.
  • An evidence log across your three strands, capturable in seconds. If logging costs more than a few taps it won't happen, and the baseline is worthless with gaps.
  • A meeting audit. Every meeting, its UK time, and a one-tap "did I need to be live?" This is the strand most likely to win the argument, because it converts a feeling into a percentage.
  • The proposal document, assembled as you go rather than written the night before. It grows from the log: the pilot terms, the coverage answer, the urgent-contact definition, the baseline numbers.
  • Objection rehearsal. The two you named, plus the ones that surface later, each with your current best answer. You want to have said these sentences before you say them to your manager.

The daily half

Same day shape as the prototype — Warm-up, Peak, Runway against the 16:00 wall — because that part was sound. What changes is the mechanics underneath.

Peak rotation, not a plan

You asked for a deliberate, enforced split. Five Peak blocks a week, assigned by pattern rather than by you deciding each morning:

PeaksGoes toWhy
2 ×/wkCampaignYour top priority, with a fixed deadline. It gets the largest share.
2 ×/wkProject choiceTime-boxed exploration, not open-ended pondering. See below.
1 ×/wkSingaporeSomething that only works because you live here. Protects the reason you want the two days at all.

Rotation is the anti-streak: miss a Monday and the pattern simply continues. Nothing breaks, so nothing is abandoned. What the app tracks is share over four weeks, which tolerates a bad week and still tells the truth about a bad month.

The slow start is the actual leak

You sleep nine hours and go straight to bed, so the morning isn't an energy problem — it's an undefended one. Waking, phone, coffee, and it's midday. The intervention is small and specific: the first action of the day is decided before the day starts, and the app asks you to name it rather than offering a menu at 09:00 when you're least able to choose.

Accountability, split the way you split it

You said a real person works for some things but you wouldn't report your whole life to them. So:

  • The bot handles the daily loop — a morning message that asks what your first action is and expects a reply, and an afternoon one that asks whether it happened. The grip comes from it noticing silence, not from a streak counter.
  • The person gets a weekly summary, and only for the domains you nominate. One message, once a week, that you send — not an integration that reports on you.

Choosing the project, as a time-boxed decision

You have several candidates and none chosen, so there is nothing to schedule. Scheduling an unchosen project is how it stays unchosen. Instead the app treats the decision as the work: list the candidates, give each a cheap test that fits one Peak block, run them, and hold a commit date. After that date one candidate takes the Peak slots and the others are explicitly parked.

What I'd build first

  1. The evidence log and meeting audit. These have a deadline the others don't — every unmeasured week is permanently missing from your baseline. Nothing else is worth building first.
  2. The morning check-in loop, in whatever channel is quickest to stand up. The daily half is worthless without the thing that expects an answer.
  3. The day view and Peak rotation, largely as prototyped.
  4. The project decision tool, with its commit date.
  5. The proposal assembler, which only becomes useful once the log has months in it.
