One Client

You asked about life admin. The admin is the easy half. The thing worth an hour of your attention is what your company's shape actually means — because it is not what I assumed, and it is better and worse than you think.

What you actually are

NEXT pays Strait Up Growth. Strait Up Growth is a Singapore Pte Ltd. It pays you a salary. So your Employment Pass is almost certainly sponsored by your own company, not by NEXT.

First: I was wrong, and in your favour

I warned you that taking client fees alongside an EP would be secondary employment needing MOM consent. That does not apply to you. That rule binds someone employed by NEXT on a NEXT-sponsored pass. You are not that. You are a Singapore company with a client, and your company can have as many clients as it can win.

Finding clients is not a legal problem. It is a sales problem, which is the one you are actually qualified for.

Second, and this is the real one

Your company has one customer. That customer's payment is your revenue, which is your salary, which is your Employment Pass, which is your right to live in Singapore with your partner. Four things hanging off one contract.

And a client can end a contract far more easily than an employer can end employment. You have no notice protection, no redundancy, none of the machinery you would have as a NEXT employee — you traded that for the structure, probably without pricing it.

So the second client is not a Plan B. It is what stops your visa depending on one company's budget cycle. That reframes the morning block entirely: it is not ambition, it is insurance.

There is a renewal dimension too. At EP renewal, MOM looks at whether the sponsoring company is a genuine operating business — commercial substance, consistent salary payment, real activity. A company with one customer and one employee is thin on paper. A second client thickens it.

October is a commercial negotiation

This is the part that should change how the next two months feel.

You have been framing October as asking your employer for different hours. You do not have an employer. You have a client, and you are their supplier. Suppliers do not request permission for their working pattern — they define a service and a price, and the client buys it or does not.

A correction to what I told you before

I said: do not bundle the rate increase with the hours change, because they will pay you and defer the hours. That was employee logic and it is wrong for your situation.

In a supplier renegotiation, scope and price belong in the same document. Splitting them is the odd move. Present one thing: here is the service definition for the next twelve months, and here is the rate. Coverage hours, response times, what is in scope, what is out. That is a normal commercial conversation with a normal commercial artefact, and it is a great deal more comfortable than asking a manager for a favour.

You are a Commercial Director. You negotiate contracts. You have been treating the single most important negotiation of your year as an HR conversation, and it is not one. Write it as a statement of work, not as a request.

The arithmetic in the other document still stands and is the substance of it: two hours of London overlap at a 19:00 Singapore finish, three at 20:00. That is the coverage clause. Everything else is commercial.

Your friend is carrying your compliance

Worth understanding properly

Your Singaporean friend is the nominated director. In Singapore, directors are personally liable for the company's filing failures. If Strait Up Growth misses ACRA or IRAS deadlines, the enforcement action — fines, and at the far end a court summons or disqualification — goes to the director. That is your friend, not you.

Corporate services firms that provide nominee directors do it with an indemnity agreement and a security deposit, precisely because of this. A friend doing you a favour almost certainly has neither.

This is the strongest argument on the page for putting a firm on retainer. Not because the filings are hard. Because the person exposed to them did not sign up for it.

The filing calendar

Everything hangs off your financial year end, which you do not currently know. Find it first — it is on your company profile in ACRA BizFile, and for a new company the first FYE must fall within 18 months of incorporation.

The one you are almost certainly missing

Skills Development Levy. Everyone assumes it is a local-employee thing. It is not: SDL is payable on everyone on payroll including Employment Pass holders, while CPF is not. You pay yourself a salary, so your company owes SDL on it.

0.25% of monthly remuneration, floor S$2, cap S$11.25 a month, due by the 14th of the following month. So this is roughly S$135 a year — trivial money and a real compliance item, which is the worst combination because nobody notices until an audit.

One nuance worth knowing: director's fees alone attract no SDL, but a director paid a monthly salary under a contract of service does. You are the second one.

A small piece of good news. A new Singapore company gets a start-up tax exemption for its first three years of assessment — 75% off the first S$100,000 of chargeable income and 50% off the next S$100,000. Worth knowing before you decide how much salary versus profit to take.

Insurance: three gaps, one urgent

Urgent
Health. NEXT's cover does not extend to you, you hold an EP, and you have nothing here. Singapore's healthcare is excellent and priced accordingly, and you are one accident away from a bill with no policy behind it. This is the single largest practical exposure in your life right now and it is the cheapest to close.
Check
Professional indemnity, territorially. You have UK indemnity cover. Most UK policies carry territorial and jurisdiction limits, and the work is now being delivered by a Singapore company, potentially to non-UK clients. Ask your insurer the direct question: does this policy respond to work performed in Singapore by a Singapore entity? If the answer is no, you are uninsured the moment Strait Up Growth takes its second client — and PI is the first thing a serious client asks to see.
Consider
Income protection. You are a one-person company. No work means no revenue, no salary, and eventually no pass. An employee who cannot work has sick pay and a job to come back to. You have neither. Your UK critical injury cover is a lump sum for catastrophe, not income replacement for six months of being unwell.

The UK side

Time-bound
National Insurance. Check your record now. From 6 April 2026 you can no longer pay voluntary Class 2 for periods abroad, so Class 3 is the route: £18.40 a week, about £957 a year. You need 35 qualifying years for the full New State Pension, currently £241.30 a week. Gaps can normally only be filled six years back, so every year you leave it, one falls off the end permanently.
Do
P85 and residence. Confirm you have filed P85 and that you are correctly non-resident under the statutory residence test. If you are in Self Assessment you will need SA109, which cannot be filed through HMRC's own online service.
Note
ISAs and pensions. You cannot contribute to an ISA while non-resident, but existing ones keep their tax status and do not close. Any pension left with a UK employer is worth locating now rather than in twenty years.

What I would actually do

You are running bookkeeping with AI, which is fine for bookkeeping and irrelevant to the problem. The problem is not that the work is hard. It is that the deadlines are annual and invisible — payroll is monthly so it gets done, and the things that carry real penalties arrive once a year with no reminder, in a country whose rules you have known for two months, with your friend's name on the liability.

Put a corporate services firm on retainer

For a one-person Pte Ltd this is typically a few hundred a month and it removes the entire category. Secretarial, filings, payroll, the tax returns, and a nominee arrangement with a proper indemnity if you want to take that weight off your friend.

By the logic of the other document, this is the obvious call: compliance is a personal goal with a deadline, which is precisely the kind of thing that fades on you. Outsourcing it does not make you weak. It moves it into the category of things that get done because someone else's job depends on it — which is the only category that has ever worked for you.
